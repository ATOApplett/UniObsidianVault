**1. CAB401 Lecture 02 – Process (Forms of Parallelism & Parallel Computing Basics)**  
Covers the landscape of parallelism at different granularities: instruction-level parallelism (superscalar, pipelining, out-of-order execution), vector parallelism (SIMD, from Cray to AVX-512), and thread/process-level parallelism (multi-threaded/multi-core CPUs, distributed processes across machines). Introduces Flynn's Taxonomy (SISD/SIMD/MISD/MIMD) and supercomputer architectures (SMP, MPP, clusters, hybrids), plus a snapshot of the current Top500 list. Defines key terms — concurrent vs. parallel vs. distributed computing — then moves into the "safe parallelization" theory: control dependencies vs. data dependencies (flow, output, anti, and input dependence), pointer aliasing hazards, and array/loop dependency analysis (e.g. matrix multiply). Ends by noting static dependence analysis is undecidable in general, so tools must be conservative, and that automatic compiler parallelization remains limited — most real parallelization is still manual, skilled, and error-prone.

**2. CAB401 Week 03 – Parallelization Process & Performance**  
Lays out the practical decision-making process: (1) determine what can safely be parallelized, (2) decide if it's worth it, (3) transform the program into explicit parallel form. Introduces granularity (coarse vs. fine-grained, and the overhead of thread creation/sync/communication that must be offset by useful work). Defines **speedup** (best sequential time ÷ parallel time) and **scalable parallelism** (speedup that keeps growing with more processors vs. a fixed constant-factor speedup). Explains that parallelism can't change a problem's computational complexity, only reduce the constant. Covers **Amdahl's Law** (max speedup = 1/((1−P)+P/S)) with the classic diminishing-returns curves. Finishes with an 11-step full methodology (profile → analyze deps → parallelize → test → re-profile) and practical guidance on timing/profiling (flat vs. call-graph profiling, gprof/VTune/Valgrind/Vampir, the "Heisenberg" effect of instrumentation distorting performance), plus the Pareto principle and Knuth's famous "premature optimization is the root of all evil" quote.

**3. CAB401 Lecture 04 – Parallel Programming Models**  
Covers how to actually map computation (and data) onto processors. Contrasts two programming models: **CSP** (Communicating Sequential Processes — independent processes, message-passing, used on distributed memory machines) vs. **PRAM** (shared-memory, constant-time access, used for thread-level parallelism). Distinguishes explicit threading (manually created/managed threads) from implicit threading (`parallel for`, `spawn`/`sync` constructs) and surveys threading libraries (Win32, Pthreads, .NET, Java). Explains thread pools as a way to amortize the cost of thread creation. Covers mapping work via iteration spaces (rectangular vs. triangular/imperfect loop nests) and load-balancing strategies: **static** (block, cyclic, block-cyclic — trading off locality vs. balance) vs. **dynamic** (work pools, over-decomposition, work-stealing — better for unpredictable workloads but higher overhead).

**4. CAB401 Week 05 – Shared Memory Concurrency & Synchronization**  
Focuses on the hazards of concurrent shared-memory access. Introduces **race conditions** with a worked example of a lost update on a shared `sum` variable. Defines mutual exclusion and its required properties (safety, liveness, fairness), then surveys the abstractions used to enforce it: mutexes, semaphores, monitors, condition variables. Walks through Dekker's algorithm as a classic lock-free mutual exclusion technique, and touches on memory consistency models (sequential consistency vs. relaxed models) and hardware primitives like `test_and_set`/`compare_and_swap`. Contrasts active (spin/busy-wait) vs. passive (sleep/event-based) waiting. Covers **deadlock** (Coffman's four necessary conditions: mutual exclusion, hold-and-wait, no preemption, circular wait) and why locks aren't composable, then **livelock**, **starvation**, and **priority inversion** as related-but-distinct failure modes. Closes with thread-safe library design and barrier synchronization for coordinating threads at fixed points.

Together, these four give you: _why/when_ to parallelize (Wk03), _the theory of what's safe_ to parallelize (Lec02), _how_ to express it in code (Lec04), and _what breaks_ when you get shared-memory access wrong (Wk05) — which maps closely onto the shared-memory IPC project you were just working through.
